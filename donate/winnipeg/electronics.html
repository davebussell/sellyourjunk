Where to donate electronics and computers in Winnipeg

5 organizations in and around Winnipeg that take donated electronics — what each one accepts, what it turns away, and which will come and collect it.

Verified 2026-09-24 from each organization's own website. We are not affiliated with anyone listed here, nobody pays to be included, and we receive nothing if you donate to them.

Nearly everything electronic in the house is worth nothing, and most of it can be dropped off free at a regulated collection site — but what is covered varies by province, Ontario's program takes information and audio-visual equipment only while British Columbia's takes far more, and Nunavut has no regulated program at all, so check your own province's accepted list before you load the car. The only question worth real time before Thursday is whether you can get into the accounts on the phones, tablets and Apple laptops: a signed-in Apple or Google account you cannot clear turns a device someone would otherwise reuse into a recycling-only item, whatever its condition.

How to tell what you have got

Before you call anyone, two minutes with the object settles most of it. None of this needs an expert — it needs a flashlight and a look at the back.

  • A matched lot of three or more working laptops of the same modelSame model number printed on the underside of each base, and each one powers on to a login screen.
  • iPhones, iPads and MacBooks from roughly 2018 onward, signed out of iCloudSettings shows no Apple Account signed in, and 'Transfer or Reset' then 'Erase All Content and Settings' runs to completion without asking for a password you do not have.
  • A desktop graphics cardOpen the side panel. Look for a long board with its own fans occupying two slot widths at the back, with a thick power cable running to it directly from the power supply. A small card with no fans and no extra power cable is not worth pulling.
  • Vintage hi-fi separates from roughly 1970 to 1985Brushed aluminum or silver front panel, real toggle switches and rotary knobs, substantial weight, and a badge reading Pioneer, Marantz, Sansui, Technics, Kenwood, Luxman, Yamaha or similar. Black plastic shelf systems and anything described as 'home theatre in a box' are not this.
  • Professional CRT monitors'PVM' or 'BVM' on the bezel, usually Sony. BNC screw-on connectors on the back rather than a coaxial antenna socket. Often no tuner and no remote.
  • Pre-1990s synthesizers, drum machines and film camera lensesSynthesizers: a keyboard covered in knobs and sliders rather than a menu screen, badged Roland, Korg, Yamaha, Moog or Sequential. Lenses: a metal mount, a ring of aperture numbers you can physically turn, and no electrical contacts.

Rarely worth anything

Not a rule, and not a reason to bin something without asking. These are the things people most often expect to be worth money and find are not.

  • CRT televisions and CRT computer monitors
  • Rear-projection and early plasma televisions
  • Printers, scanners, fax machines and all-in-one devices
  • Any phone, tablet or Mac still locked to an account you cannot clear
  • DVD players, set-top boxes, shelf stereos and home-theatre-in-a-box systems
  • Cables, chargers, keyboards, mice, routers, modems and loose power bricks
  • Monitors under about 24 inches, older LCD panels, and loose optical discs
  • Landline telephones, answering machines and corded office phones
Free consumer drop-off is a producer obligation, not a courtesy Ontario

Under the Electrical and Electronic Equipment regulation, producers of information technology, telecommunications and audio-visual equipment have been individually accountable since 1 January 2021 for collecting and reusing, refurbishing or recycling their products, and must ensure consumers can drop off covered equipment free of charge at each site in their collection system. The Resource Productivity and Recovery Authority is the regulator. The previous Waste Electrical and Electronic Equipment program ended on 31 December 2020.

Ontario's program covers information equipment only, not appliances or tools Ontario

The regulation covers equipment whose primary purpose is collecting, storing, processing, presenting or communicating information, sounds and images, plus supporting peripherals, cables and charging equipment. It excludes small and large appliances and power tools.

Read the rule →
British Columbia accepts a far wider list, including tube displays British Columbia

The program lists 25-plus categories including kitchen countertop appliances, floor care, garment care, power tools, personal care and beauty, exercise machines, medical and monitoring equipment, e-bikes and e-scooters, and musical instruments. The Display Devices category expressly includes cathode ray tube televisions and monitors. Computer peripherals cover keyboards and mice, IT and telecom equipment covers routers and modems, and cables are listed as accepted when bundled with a computer. The FAQ states residents and businesses can drop off unwanted electronics free of charge.

Read the rule →
Responsibility for erasing personal data sits with the owner, not the recycler All EPRA provinces

EPRA states that consumers and businesses are responsible for ensuring all personal data is removed from their devices before bringing them to a drop-off location. Its pre-drop-off guidance is to deactivate the device's account service, clear all personal information, and remove the SIM card, and it directs people to manufacturer documentation, erasure software, or expert help. EPRA requires its processors to protect data but does not accept responsibility for information left on a device.

Read the rule →
Overwriting works on spinning disks and does not work on solid-state drives Canada, federal guidance

The Canadian Centre for Cyber Security's ITSP.40.006 states that a single overwrite pass is effective for modern hard disk drives, but that overwriting and secure erase processes are not suitable for most flash-based devices such as solid-state drives, because wear-levelling features prevent tools from reaching all memory. Cryptographic erase — destroying the encryption key — is identified as suitable for encrypted hard drives, solid-state drives and other flash storage, provided encryption was in use throughout the device's life. Physical destruction should be preceded by best-effort encryption or erasure, so that data cannot be recovered from fragments.

Windows' built-in data erasure is adequate for household data and not for sensitive data Not jurisdictional — vendor statement applicable throughout Canada

Microsoft recommends Reset this PC with Remove everything and the data erasure option enabled before donating, recycling or selling, describing it as removing files and cleaning the drive and taking an hour or two. Microsoft also states that this consumer-targeted erasure does not meet government and industry data erasure standards.

Read the rule →
Activation Lock comes off with the erase, and can be cleared remotely after the device has left your hands Not jurisdictional — Apple policy applicable in Canada

Apple's instructions are to sign out of iCloud and then run Transfer or Reset, Erase All Content and Settings. The same page states that when you erase the device, Find My and Activation Lock turn off, and that if Find My was turned on you might need to enter your Apple Account and password — so the account password, not the order of operations, is what determines whether the device can be cleared. If Find My was left on and the device is gone, the owner can sign in at icloud.com/find, select the device, erase it, then remove it from the device list. If the device is offline, the erase begins the next time it connects.

Read the rule →
Clearing a deceased owner's Apple Account requires legal documentation Canada

Apple requires legal documentation before it will assist with a deceased person's account. This generally includes a death certificate, and might also require a court order. A Legacy Contact access key, if one was set up, is the simplest route and may be filed with estate papers. Apple states that after a Legacy Contact request or an account deletion request is completed, Activation Lock is removed automatically.

Read the rule →
Apple will consider an Activation Lock removal request supported by proof of purchase Not jurisdictional — Apple policy applicable in Canada

Apple's Activation Lock page states that if you need help removing Activation Lock and have proof of purchase documentation, you can start an Activation Lock support request. The page does not set out what that documentation must contain, so the criteria are whatever Apple applies through the request process — do not assume a particular receipt or a photograph of the device will qualify.

Read the rule →
EPRA runs the program in eight provinces; Alberta and Newfoundland and Labrador have their own operators Canada

EPRA states it is fulfilling this role on behalf of obligated stewards in eight provinces: British Columbia, Saskatchewan, Manitoba, Ontario, Quebec, New Brunswick, Nova Scotia and Prince Edward Island. In Ontario it operates inside the individual-producer-responsibility regime that the Resource Productivity and Recovery Authority regulates. Alberta's program is administered by the Alberta Recycling Management Authority and Newfoundland and Labrador's by the Multi-Materials Stewardship Board. EPRA does not operate in the territories.

Read the rule →
Coverage in the territories is partial, and Nunavut has none Yukon, Northwest Territories, Nunavut

The Northwest Territories runs depots including Yellowknife, N'Dilo, Dettah and Hay River, with an expanded list of more than 500 products across seven categories: small appliances and lighting, audio visual, telecom devices, power and air tools, games, toys and music, lawn and garden, and solar panels. Yukon operates a consumer-funded electronics recycling program funded by fees at point of purchase. Nunavut has no regulated electronics stewardship program. Whether a tube television is accepted, and at what cost, could not be confirmed at depot level for either territory, so confirm with the specific depot.
