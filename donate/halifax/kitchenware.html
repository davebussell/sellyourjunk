Where to donate kitchenware and housewares in Halifax

5 organizations in and around Halifax that take donated kitchenware — what each one accepts, what it turns away, and which will come and collect it.

How to tell what you have got

Before you call anyone, two minutes with the object settles most of it. None of this needs an expert — it needs a flashlight and a look at the back.

  • Sterling silver flatware and hollowwareEach piece is stamped STERLING, STER, 925, or carries a British lion passant. EPNS, EP, A1, nickel silver, or the words silver plate mean there is no metal value at all. Knives usually have hollow handles filled with pitch or resin and a stainless blade, and weighted candlesticks and shakers are filled with pitch, so do not count their full weight as silver.
  • Bare cast iron frying pans and Dutch ovensSmooth, not pebbled, cooking surface, and raised letters on the base — Griswold, Wagner, Favorite, or the Century and Red Mountain marks used by Birmingham Stove & Range. Hairline cracks, a base that rocks on a flat counter, or deep pitting through the metal kill the value entirely. Surface rust does not.
  • Le Creuset and Staub enamelled cast ironBrand name cast into the base with a size number. Check the interior enamel for chips and crazing before you price anything — it is the first thing a buyer looks at. A missing lid takes a large bite out of the price; sold listings for your size, with and without the lid, will show you how much.
  • KitchenAid stand mixers, Vitamix and Thermomix blenders, and Breville or Jura espresso machinesPlug it in and run it before you price it or load it. The model number is on the base or the back. Original bowls, blades, dough hooks and attachments matter more than cosmetic condition.
  • Patterned vintage Pyrex opalwareOpaque white glass with a printed pattern — Butterprint, Gooseberry, Snowflake, or the primary-colour nesting bowls — and a Pyrex mark on the base. Dishwasher-faded patterns, lost lids and chips to the rim all cut what a piece will fetch; by how much is not published. Pyrex was genuinely made in Canada, by Corning Glass Works of Canada at Leamington, Ontario, so a Canadian mark is normal and not in itself a premium.
  • Branded kitchen knives in a matched setWüsthof, Zwilling J.A. Henckels with the two-figure logo, Global, or a Japanese maker's mark on the blade. A single Henckels figure, a bent tip, a wobbling bolster, or a blade reground into a crescent means it is scrap.
  • Heavy copper and commercial-grade cookwareReal copper is heavy for its size and the metal is thick at the rim — a thin copper-coloured skin over aluminum is decorative only. Mauviel and Falk are stamped. Commercial pieces are unbranded, heavy, and usually scratched to death, which does not matter.

Rarely worth anything

Not a rule, and not a reason to bin something without asking. These are the things people most often expect to be worth money and find are not.

  • Complete bone china dinner services — Royal Albert, Royal Doulton, Wedgwood, Noritake, Paragon
  • Cut crystal stemware, decanters and fruit bowls
  • Anything silver-plated — EPNS trays, tea services, cutlery, cake stands
  • Non-stick pans with scratched, flaking or worn coating
  • Chipped, cracked or crazed dishes and mugs
  • Incomplete sets — seven of twelve plates, a casserole with no lid, four glasses from a set of eight
  • Commemorative and collector plates, souvenir mugs, promotional glassware
  • Plastic food storage, mismatched containers and lidless Tupperware
  • Burnt bakeware, rusted tins, warped pots, worn wooden and plastic utensils, opened food and part-used cleaning products
Leachable lead and cadmium limits apply to glazed ceramics and glassware sold in Canada Canada (federal)

The Glazed Ceramics and Glassware Regulations, SOR/2016-175, made under the Canada Consumer Product Safety Act, set maximum migration limits: lead 3.0 mg/L for flatware, 2.0 mg/L for small hollowware, 1.0 mg/L for large hollowware, 0.5 mg/L for cups and mugs, and 0.5 mg/L for pitchers, with cadmium limits of 0.25 to 0.50 mg/L depending on the item. The practical consequence for a house clearance is that worn, crazed and chipped glazes leach more than intact ones, and leaching rises with heat, acidity and contact time — which is both a reason not to keep using damaged dishes and the reason charities refuse them. These regulations replaced the earlier Glazed Ceramics and Glassware Regulations, SOR/98-176, which were repealed in 2016.

Giving a recalled product away is prohibited on the same terms as selling it Canada (federal)

Section 8 of the Canada Consumer Product Safety Act (S.C. 2010, c. 21) prohibits any person from advertising or selling a consumer product they know is the subject of a recall order or a voluntary recall in Canada. Section 2 defines "sell" to include distributing a product to one or more persons "whether or not the distribution is made for consideration", so handing a recalled appliance to a neighbour, a thrift shop or a buy-nothing group for free is caught by the same rule. For a kitchen clearance that makes checking model numbers against Health Canada's recalls and safety alerts database a legal step rather than a precaution, and it applies to the small appliances most people assume are too trivial to check.

Small kitchen appliances are covered by a free producer-funded recycling program in British Columbia British Columbia

Portable electrical appliances for food preparation with heating elements or motors — kettles, toasters, blenders, coffee makers — plus vacuums, sewing machines, power tools and countertop microwaves are accepted at Recycle My Electronics depots, operated by the Electronic Products Recycling Association. The recycling cost is collected as an Environmental Handling Fee at the point of purchase rather than at drop-off. Built-in appliances and large stationary equipment such as built-in microwaves and air conditioners are not in the program. This program absorbed what BC residents previously knew as ElectroRecycle; the old electrorecycle.ca address now redirects to Recycle My Electronics BC.

Ontario's electronics regulation does not cover small kitchen appliances Ontario

O. Reg. 522/20 under the Resource Recovery and Circular Economy Act, 2016 applies to information technology, telecommunications and audio-visual equipment and to lighting. ITT/AV is defined by its primary purpose of collecting, storing, processing, presenting or communicating information, sounds or images. A kettle, toaster, blender or microwave is none of those things, so there is no producer-funded take-back for them in Ontario. The route is your municipality's depot or transfer station, usually as scrap metal. Do not assume a BC or American program applies here.

Saskatchewan expanded its electronics stewardship program to include small appliances in 2025 Saskatchewan

Extended producer responsibility bulletins report that the Electronic Equipment Stewardship Program Regulations came into force on 4 September 2025 under The Environmental Management and Protection Act, 2010, bringing small appliances into the regulated program, with EPRA to communicate the expanded product list during 2026. This is drawn from a law-firm EPR summary rather than the regulation text, which could not be opened. Confirm with EPRA Saskatchewan what is accepted before driving anywhere with a car full of small appliances.
