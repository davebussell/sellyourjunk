How it works

Simple on purpose.

Every extra field is a reason not to post. So there are almost none.

If you're selling it

  • Take a photo. We identify it properly — make, model, condition, faults.
  • You get two numbers, never one: what it fetches in a private sale, and what a buyer will hand you today. Buyers pay less. We say so.
  • You see the buyers who want that specific thing — what they've bought before and what they pay.
  • They can see your photos and your neighbourhood. Not your name, address or number — not until you pick one and say yes.
  • One item, one approved buyer. You will not get fourteen phone calls.
  • It costs you nothing. The buyer pays us, and only when we actually deliver.

If you're giving it away

  • Take a photo. We write the listing.
  • Say when it has to be gone.
  • Turn on Porch Pickup if you'd rather not be home — leave it out, someone takes it, done.
  • Add a line about the thing if you want to. People claim items with stories far more often.
  • Charities get 24 hours first, unless you're in a hurry. One tap opens it to everyone.
  • We'll tell you if it's worth money before you give it away. Plenty of people give it away anyway. That's a good outcome too.

If you want something

  • Browse what's near you. Everything shows how long is left.
  • Claiming means picking an actual arrival window — not "I'm interested".
  • The owner approves your time, then the address arrives. You message through a relay, so neither of you hands a stranger a phone number.
  • Miss your window and it passes to the next person automatically. No awkward messages.
  • No haggling, no chat threads, no "is this still available?" The countdown answers that.
  • It's free. There's no catch, no membership, and nobody checks whether you qualify.

Things we decided not to build

No messaging. Chat is where these platforms die — haggling, ghosting, "is this still available?". Buyers send one number and you tap yes or no. Everyone else gets four buttons: on my way, running late, can't make it, got it.

No fees to you. Not a listing fee, not a commission, not a cut of what a buyer pays you. The buyers fund this, and only when we put a real seller in front of them. If we don't deliver, we don't get paid either.

No volunteer moderators. The most consistent failure of every neighbourhood giving group. Moderation here is automated first and paid staff second. No neighbour will ever have the power to ban another neighbour.

No eligibility checks. We don't ask why you want it or whether you need it. A large share of people using this are under real financial pressure and none of them should have to prove it to get a lamp.

No reseller police. Someone who takes a free dresser and flips it has still kept a dresser out of the ground and put it in a home. Givers can set a preference and we honour it — but we're not building an inquisition.

Questions people actually ask

Before you photograph anything.

What does it cost me?
Nothing, ever. No listing fee, no commission, no cut of what a buyer pays you. Commercial buyers — dealers, recyclers, restorers — pay us to reach people who are selling things, and only when we actually put a real seller in front of them. You are never the one being charged.
Do I need an account?
No. Photograph something and you'll get a valuation without signing up, without an email address and without a password. We only ask for a way to reach you at the point where a buyer wants to arrange collection — and you can stop before that.
Who gets my address and phone number?
Nobody, until you pick a specific buyer and say yes. Buyers see your photos, what the item is and your neighbourhood — never your name, street address or number. When you approve one, that buyer gets your details and no one else does. Calls and messages run through a relay that switches off after collection, so your real number never leaves us.
What if my stuff isn't worth anything?
We'll tell you straight away, in the first sentence. Plenty of things are worth nothing — most upholstered sofas, most exercise equipment — and an old upright piano will usually cost you $200–400 to remove. We'd rather say so before you spend time photographing it than after.
What happens if nobody wants to buy it?
It rolls into the give-away list automatically, with charities and community groups getting first look for 24 hours because they have trucks and volunteers. Nothing sits in limbo, and nothing you post is left without a next step.
Do I have to be home for the pickup?
Not for give-aways — turn on Porch Pickup, leave it outside, and whoever gets there first takes it. For a sale you'll usually want to be there to hand it over and take payment, but you pick the window and the buyer works around it.
Which areas do you cover?
We're starting deliberately small in the western GTA — Mississauga, Etobicoke and Brampton — because a marketplace like this only works when a buyer can actually get to you and back. Valuations work anywhere; buyer matching depends on someone being near enough to collect.
What sort of things can I photograph?
Anything you're trying to get rid of. Furniture, appliances, bikes, tools, electronics, scrap metal, instruments, garden equipment, a car that stopped running, boxes you haven't opened since you moved. If we can't identify it confidently we'll say so rather than guess at a number.
For organizations

We lower your cost per tonne.

You already know what you need. What you don't know is which house three streets over is about to throw it in a bin. That's the only thing we do.

What you get

  • Photographed, categorised, measured items — before you send a truck
  • A standing wants list you set once, then never think about again
  • 24 hours of exclusive access before anything opens to the public
  • Geographically batched pickups, so one run collects from several homes
  • Claim whole lots — "all the fabric", "the dining set" — not item by item

What it costs

Nothing.

Not now, not later, not for premium features or priority placement. The moment a shelter has to weigh a subscription fee against its programs, we've broken the thing we're trying to do.

Commercial buyers — dealers, recyclers, restorers — pay us to reach people selling things. That business funds this one. You are not the product being sold, and you never compete with a paying buyer for an item: the person giving it away chooses you first.

Our story

Nothing useful should end up in a landfill because nobody knew it was there.

When my grandparents died, my dad had a few days to empty their house. The couches went in a dumpster. So did the sewing machines, the fabric, the lamps.

None of it was worthless. My grandmother had sewn on that machine for decades. Somebody, somewhere within a few kilometres of that house, needed a lamp and a kitchen table and could not afford one. There was no way to find them in three days, so a lifetime of useful things went into a hole in the ground.

Watching it happen was heartbreaking, and the part I couldn't stop thinking about afterwards was that nothing about it was necessary. This isn't a shortage problem. The things exist. The people who need them exist. They're often on the same street on the same afternoon. They just never meet.

More than half of the bulky waste we bury is still perfectly usable. We're not running out of things. We're running out of ways to find each other in time.

Facebook groups and giving boards half-solve this, and I'm grateful they exist. But they're gated, fragmented, and full of listings that have sat there since March. You have to join, be approved, learn the rules, tolerate the drama — and then still get ghosted by three people who said they'd come.

So this works differently. You say what you have and when it has to be gone. That deadline is the entire mechanic. It's honest — it's your actual constraint — and it gives a charity with a van something they can genuinely plan around. When the clock runs out, the listing disappears. Nothing here gets stale, because nothing here can.

Charities, shelters and community groups get first look, because they have trucks and volunteers where most of us have a sedan and good intentions. Then it opens to everyone, and we never ask anybody to prove they need what they're taking.

Then there's the part that surprised me, and it's why this site is called what it's called. Some of it is worth real money. A dealer will pay for that sewing machine. A restorer will drive an hour for the right old bicycle. Those people spend their weekends hunting for exactly the things the rest of us are about to put at the curb — and they'll pay to be told where it is. That's the whole business. They pay; you never do; and their money is what keeps the free side free.

So we always tell you both numbers: what a buyer will put in your hand this week, and what you'd get selling it yourself if you had the patience. Buyers pay less. We say so on the screen, because you'll find out at the doorstep otherwise. And when the honest answer is that a sewing programme three streets away needs it more than you need forty dollars, we say that too — even though we earn nothing when you give something away. Something going somewhere beats it going nowhere.

And for anyone facing what my dad faced — a house, a deadline, and a lifetime of belongings — we built something specific. Walk through the rooms with your phone. We'll list everything, sort out who wants what, and hand you a schedule so you're not answering the door forty times in a weekend. At the end you get a page that tells you where everything went.

My grandparents' things are gone. But there's a house being emptied somewhere this week with the same things in it, and right now the only tool that family has is a bin.
