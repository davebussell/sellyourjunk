How it works

Simple on purpose.

Every extra field is a reason not to post. So there are almost none.

If you're giving something away

  • Take a photo. We write the listing.
  • Say when it has to be gone.
  • Turn on Porch Pickup if you'd rather not be home — leave it out, someone takes it, done.
  • Add a line about the thing if you want to. People claim items with stories far more often.
  • Charities get 24 hours first, unless you're in a hurry. One tap opens it to everyone.

If you want something

  • Browse what's near you. Everything shows how long is left.
  • Claiming means picking an actual arrival window — not "I'm interested".
  • The owner approves your time, then the address arrives. You message through a relay, so neither of you hands a stranger a phone number.
  • Miss your window and it passes to the next person automatically. No awkward messages.
  • No haggling, no chat threads, no "is this still available?" The countdown answers that.
  • It's free. There's no catch, no membership, and nobody checks whether you qualify.

Things we decided not to build

No messaging. Chat is where these platforms die — negotiation over free items, ghosting, arguments. You get four buttons: on my way, running late, can't make it, got it.

No volunteer moderators. The most consistent failure of every neighbourhood giving group. Moderation here is automated first and paid staff second. No neighbour will ever have the power to ban another neighbour.

No eligibility checks. We don't ask why you want it or whether you need it. A large share of people using this are under real financial pressure and none of them should have to prove it to get a lamp.

No reseller police. Someone who takes a free dresser and flips it has still kept a dresser out of the ground and put it in a home. Givers can set a preference and we honour it — but we're not building an inquisition.

For organizations

We lower your cost per tonne.

You already know what you need. What you don't know is which house three streets over is about to throw it in a bin. That's the only thing we do.

What you get

  • Photographed, categorised, measured items — before you send a truck
  • A standing wants list you set once, then never think about again
  • 24 hours of exclusive access before anything opens to the public
  • Geographically batched pickups, so one run collects from several homes
  • Claim whole lots — "all the fabric", "the dining set" — not item by item
  • An automatic monthly impact report you can forward to your board

What it costs

Nothing.

Not now, not later, not for premium features or priority placement. The moment a shelter has to weigh a subscription fee against its programs, we've broken the thing we're trying to do.

We're funded by grants, municipal diversion contracts, and referral fees from the haulers who take away what nobody claimed.

Our story

Nothing useful should end up in a landfill because nobody knew it was there.

When my grandparents died, my dad had a few days to empty their house. The couches went in a dumpster. So did the sewing machines, the fabric, the lamps.

None of it was worthless. My grandmother had sewn on that machine for decades. Somebody, somewhere within a few kilometres of that house, needed a lamp and a kitchen table and could not afford one. There was no way to find them in three days, so a lifetime of useful things went into a hole in the ground.

Watching it happen was heartbreaking, and the part I couldn't stop thinking about afterwards was that nothing about it was necessary. This isn't a shortage problem. The things exist. The people who need them exist. They're often on the same street on the same afternoon. They just never meet.

More than half of the bulky waste we bury is still perfectly usable. We're not running out of things. We're running out of ways to find each other in time.

Facebook groups and giving boards half-solve this, and I'm grateful they exist. But they're gated, fragmented, and full of listings that have sat there since March. You have to join, be approved, learn the rules, tolerate the drama — and then still get ghosted by three people who said they'd come.

So this works differently. You say what you have and when it has to be gone. That deadline is the entire mechanic. It's honest — it's your actual constraint — and it gives a charity with a van something they can genuinely plan around. When the clock runs out, the listing disappears. Nothing here gets stale, because nothing here can.

Charities, shelters and community groups get first look, because they have trucks and volunteers where most of us have a sedan and good intentions. Then it opens to everyone, and we never ask anybody to prove they need what they're taking.

And for anyone facing what my dad faced — a house, a deadline, and a lifetime of belongings — we built something specific. Walk through the rooms with your phone. We'll list everything, sort out who wants what, and hand you a schedule so you're not answering the door forty times in a weekend. At the end you get a page that tells you where everything went.

My grandparents' things are gone. But there's a house being emptied somewhere this week with the same things in it, and right now the only tool that family has is a bin.
