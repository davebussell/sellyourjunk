How to tell what you have got

Before you call anyone, two minutes with the object settles most of it. None of this needs an expert — it needs a flashlight and a look at the back.

  • LPs in almost any genre except classical, opera and overstocked easy listeningLook up the exact pressing's completed sales on Discogs, not its asking prices. Audiopile says it reads the median price rather than the highest, also looks at the "Wants" count to judge sellability, and warns that online prices do not necessarily reflect the local market. Pressing origin matters — it pays more for Japanese, UK and other imports — so read the catalogue and matrix numbers on the label and in the run-out groove, not just the artist.
  • Records you were about to throw out because they look roughPut the visibly damaged records in a separate box and take them along anyway rather than sorting them into the bin.
  • Antiquarian books, modern firsts, fine bindings and local historyLook for a dust jacket present and intact, a date on the title page matching the copyright page with no later printing line, and an author signature. With none of those it is probably an ordinary secondhand book — but anything pre-1900, finely bound, or local history should be seen by a dealer before you decide.
  • Books published in the last five years, and classics in new editionsCheck the copyright page. If the newest date is more than five years old and the book is nonfiction, Halifax is not the stop for it — and its cap is 20 items per household in any case.
  • Children's and teen books printed since 2000, in gift conditionIt specifically wants board and baby books, preschool titles, popular series, graphic novels, non-fiction on nature, animals and space, and books with diverse characters. Its refusal list is short and exact: textbooks, encyclopedias, workbooks, library books, self-published books and damaged books.
  • CDs with no scratches, with their original linersA disc in a wallet or binder that still has its liner meets Halifax's rule. Discs stripped of their artwork are the hard ones to place.
  • Cassettes, where they are collectible rather than commonAudiopile's distinction is between common commercial tapes and collectible ones; a tape recorded or dubbed at home is neither. Sealed or small-run commercial releases are the ones worth asking about.

Rarely worth anything

Not a rule, and not a reason to bin something without asking. These are the things people most often expect to be worth money and find are not.

  • General mass-market paperbacks
  • Reader's Digest Condensed Books and other book-club condensed editions
  • Encyclopedia sets
  • Ex-library books and library discards
  • Outdated textbooks and computer manuals
  • Travel guides
  • Magazines, including long runs of National Geographic
  • Classical and opera LPs
  • Easy-listening and middle-of-the-road LPs
  • VHS tapes and ordinary audio cassettes
  • Used DVDs and Blu-rays, as a sale
  • Anything mouldy, musty, water-damaged, yellowed or heavily highlighted
  • Self-published books and workbooks
  • Hardcovers and spiral-bound books, as a prison donation
Prison book programs take softcovers only, and screen the content Canada — correctional institutions, via Books to Prisoners BC (Vancouver)

Books to Prisoners BC says that most prisons in Canada do not accept hardcover books, so it prefers that you do not donate them; it adds that hardcovers weigh more and cost more to ship. Spiral-bound books are also on its not-accepted list, along with damaged books, pornography, anything explaining how to immobilize a person, Canadian true crime about people currently incarcerated, and books critical of Canadian police and prison systems. Do not mail books to a prisoner directly on the assumption they will arrive.

Calgary instructs residents to remove cardboard hard covers and spines and recycle them as separate items from the pages, and to put coverless and loose pages into the recycling. Leather, canvas and plastic book covers go in the black cart as garbage. Old and unusable paper books may go in the blue cart or a community recycling depot.

Calgary directs old and broken discs and tapes — vinyl records, CDs, DVDs and video tapes — to the black cart as garbage, while paper covers, music liners and record sleeves go in the blue cart. All of the following was verified against primary sources fetched directly: The Pawned Property (Recording) Act, c P-4.2 and The Pawned Property (Recording) Regulations, c P-4.2 Reg 1 (both downloaded from the Office of the King's Printer at publications.saskatchewan.ca), and The Business License Bylaw, 2002, Bylaw No. 8075, codified to Bylaw No. 9684, August 31 2020 (downloaded from saskatoon.ca). WHO THE PROVINCIAL RULES BIND. This is the point that decides whether the rest applies to a given shop. Section 2(e) of the Act defines a pawnbroker as \"a person who regularly conducts more than 10 pawn transactions per month\". The Act binds pawnbrokers. It does not impose identification or police-reporting duties on every second-hand dealer in the province, so an ordinary used-electronics shop, record shop, bookshop or jeweller that is not a pawnbroker is outside it. Section 2(g) defines an \"unconditional sale\" as the purchase of an article by a pawnbroker outright - so when a pawnbroker buys something outright, that sale is caught by the Act too, not just the loans. One further condition: section 7(4) says the identification and reporting section applies to a pawnbroker only where the chief of police has given at least 90 days' notice; in Saskatoon, bylaw section 27.7 has required electronic recording and reporting of all pawn transactions since January 1, 2005. IDENTIFICATION - TWO PIECES, ONE WITH A PHOTOGRAPH. Regulation section 4(1) requires a pawnbroker, before a pawn transaction or an unconditional sale, to obtain your name, address, telephone number and date of birth; a description of you including height, weight, gender, hair colour and eye colour; and the identification record numbers from two pieces of identification. One of the two must be one of the following types and only counts if it carries your photograph: a valid driver's licence issued by a Canadian province or territory or a US state; a passport issued by the government of origin; a Certificate of Indian Status Card issued by the Government of Canada; a Certificate of Citizenship Card issued by the Government of Canada; or a Conditional Release Card issued by Correctional Services of Canada. An original birth certificate may be used only if the accompanying second piece carries your photograph. Section 7(2) of the Act sets the consequence, quoted exactly: if a person \"refuses or is unable to provide the information required pursuant to subsection (1), the pawnbroker shall not complete the pawn transaction or unconditional sale.\" SERIAL NUMBERS. Regulation section 5 bars a pawnbroker from taking an article that bears or customarily bears a serial number \"unless the serial number is clearly legible and has not been totally or partially altered, removed or obscured.\" WHAT GETS RECORDED AND WHO SEES IT. Regulation section 4(1)(d) requires a detailed description of the article - manufacturer, year of manufacture, make and model, serial number, identifying marks, any title on the item, the media type for a CD, tape, vinyl record, DVD or VHS, and for jewellery the type, weight, karat, engraving or micro-reference, and the number, type, colour, clarity and shape of each gemstone. Section 4(2) requires the date and time, the price paid or the amount loaned on pawn, and the name of the person conducting the transaction; the dealer must issue you a receipt and retain a hard copy signed by both of you. Regulation section 6 requires the pawnbroker to record this electronically in a format approved by the chief of police immediately after the transaction, provide it to the chief of police as soon as practicable, and file a report at least once each day the shop is open - including a nil report on days with no transactions. A pawn or an outright sale at a pawnshop is therefore an identified, police-reported transaction. EXEMPTIONS - MOST HOUSE-CLEAROUT GOODS ARE OUTSIDE THE ACT. Regulation section 3(f) lists articles the Act does not apply to: second-hand books, magazines, journals, periodicals and other similar printed materials; postage stamps; second-hand infant's, toddler's or children's clothing, shoes, equipment, furniture or safety devices; adult clothing or shoes except leather or fur jackets and coats; second-hand cookware, glassware and eating utensils that do not contain precious metals; waste paper, waste cardboard and waste glass; second-hand furniture; second-hand appliances including refrigerators, freezers, stoves, washers, dryers, dishwashers and garbage compactors; and non-motorized lawn or garden equipment and tools. Section 3 also exempts retail returns for refund, exchange or credit, and acquisitions from a law enforcement agency, from a merchant, retailer or wholesaler with a permanent place of business, from a trustee in bankruptcy, executor, administrator or receiver who shows proof of position, or from a public official acting under judicial authority. SASKATOON'S BYLAW - PART V, PAWNBROKERS. Bylaw 8075 defines a \"pawnshop\" as \"a business wherein more than 10 pawn transactions per month are conducted\" and adopts the Act's definitions of pawnbroker, pawn transaction and unconditional sale. Section 27.2 requires a licence to operate a pawnshop, renewed annually. Section 27.3 bars a pawnshop from being located within 160 metres of another, measured property line to property line, with grandfathering for pawnshops that held a valid licence on October 4, 2004. Section 27.4 sets holding periods: a pawnbroker \"shall hold any property received in a pawn transaction or unconditional sale for at least two consecutive clear business days\", shall not display it for sale for 30 days, and shall not sell it until it has been displayed for sale for at least 15 days after that 30-day period. Section 27.5: a pawnbroker \"shall not engage in a pawn transaction or conditional sale with a person under the age of 18 years.\" Section 27.6: a pawnbroker \"shall not accept personal identification in a pawn transaction\" - your ID document itself cannot be the pawned article. Section 27.7 requires electronic recording and reporting of all pawn transactions under the provincial Act, effective January 1, 2005. PAWNING VERSUS SELLING. Both happen at the same counter and the paperwork looks similar, so the distinction is worth stating plainly. A pawn transaction is defined in the Act as depositing an article with a pawnbroker at interest or in expectation of profit, where it is express or implied that the article may be redeemed or repurchased. You get cash now, the shop holds the item, and you get it back only by repaying the advance plus the lender's charges by the deadline. Miss the deadline and the item is forfeited and sold, and the cash you took is the whole of what you received. An unconditional sale is final on the day. None of the Saskatoon pawnshops whose sites were checked publish interest rates, fees or redemption periods anywhere online, so those figures exist only in the store.
